\section{Identification}
\label{sec:identification}

We now describe where the knowledge of the system comes from, which
diagnostic goals it gives rise to, which requirements the system has to
satisfy, and which reasoning strategy was chosen to satisfy them.


\subsection{Knowledge Acquisition}

The knowledge base is taken from the text of the DSM-5-TR~\cite{dsm}, chapter
by chapter, and from nothing else: no case material of the manual or of a
casebook was used. The clinical judgment that the manual leaves to the
clinician (e.g., whether a worry is ``excessive'', or whether a disturbance is
``better explained by another mental disorder'') is left to the clinician also
in PESAD, in the form of a question. For each chapter, a \emph{criteria sheet}
(the files \code{doc/criteria\_sheet\_*.md} of the repository) lists the
disorders with their criteria paraphrased in my words, the letters, counts,
durations and codes being those of the manual, and with the clause of the
knowledge base that implements each criterion. These sheets were checked against
the DSM-5-TR text criterion by criterion; a reader can use them to verify (or
dispute) the encoding without reading Prolog. As for the ICD-11
codes, they come from the browser of the ICD-11 for Mortality and Morbidity
Statistics~\cite{icd11}; the cutoffs of the tests accepted by the evaluation
from test results (section~\ref{sec:evaluate}) come from the manuals of the
instruments and from the DSM-5-TR text.

The criteria of a disorder define a single ``parent'' diagnosis, whereas what a
clinician records is a code. A code often identifies the parent diagnosis
together with a specifier or a subtype (e.g., specific phobia, animal type,
\code{F40.218}; adjustment disorder with anxiety, \code{F43.22}; ADHD,
predominantly inattentive presentation, \code{F90.0}). PESAD therefore uses as
\emph{diagnostic goals} the codable diagnoses, i.e., the parent disorders
together with their separately coded specifiers and subtypes. Also the
specifiers that the manual requires to be recorded, although they are not coded
separately (e.g., the insight specifier of obsessive-compulsive disorder, or the
level of support of autism), are goals, so that the result of a consultation
names them.
Table~\ref{tab:disorders} lists the 92 goals obtained in this way. Two
specifiers are not modeled: the panic attack specifier, which, according to the DSM-5-TR,
cannot be coded, and the specifiers of autism on associated conditions and
catatonia.

Preschool posttraumatic stress disorder (children 6 years and younger) is not a
separate goal: it is a branch of the rules of posttraumatic stress disorder,
selected by the age band, which applies the lowered thresholds of the preschool
subtype. Similarly, the age-dependent durations and thresholds of separation
anxiety disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, social anxiety disorder,
prolonged grief disorder and ADHD are branches of the same goal, selected by the
age band of the patient.


\subsection{Requirements}

\subsubsection{Programming Language}

The system is implemented in Prolog~\cite{sterling-shapiro}, a logic
programming language in which the diagnostic knowledge can be written almost
literally as definite clauses (``a disorder holds if its criteria hold and its
exclusions do not''), and whose resolution procedure gives backward chaining and
backtracking at no cost. Two behaviors required by the system are not provided
by Prolog, and they are implemented on top of it: the treatment of uncertainty
and the explanation of the reasoning.

\subsubsection{Treatment of Uncertainty}

Prolog reasons categorically, a goal being either proved or not. In medical
diagnosis, however, the knowledge is often uncertain, both because a symptom may
be only probably associated with a disorder and because the clinician may not be
completely sure of an answer (e.g., whether a worry has lasted six months, or
whether a disturbance is better explained by another disorder). It is therefore
required that an answer can be given with a degree of certainty in $[0,1]$,
that a rule carry a certainty of its own, and that the certainty of a diagnosis
be computed from both. Certainty is meant here as the subjective confidence of
the expert in an assertion or in the applicability of a rule, in the sense of
the certainty factors of MYCIN~\cite{shortliffe-buchanan}. A calculus that is
local (the certainty of a conclusion depends only on those of its premises and
of its rule) and easily explained was preferred to a probabilistic model, which
would require joint distributions that are not available for this domain (cf.
the discussion in~\cite{heckerman}).
Furthermore, since the choice of the operator that combines the premises of a
rule is, to a large extent, arbitrary, it is required that the operator can be
chosen among several t-norms, and that the validation be run under all of them.

\subsubsection{Explanation Module}

Users are, quite reasonably, reluctant to trust a conclusion produced by a
procedure they cannot inspect. This is all the more true in medical diagnosis,
where an explanation also allows the clinician to find an error in the
knowledge base or in the answers. Explanations are required on two
occasions: for \emph{why} a question is posed, by giving the
diagnostic reason behind it, and for \emph{how} a conclusion was
reached, by showing the proof tree with the certainty of each node.


\subsection{Reasoning Strategy}

The inference is \emph{backward chaining}: one starts from a candidate
diagnosis and tries to prove it by applying the rules from the conclusions to
the premises, down to the elementary facts. These are requested from the user
only when they are needed, and only once, since the answers are stored in the
working memory. Forward chaining, which starts from the known facts and
derives everything they imply, would require all the facts in advance, which is
precisely what a consultation does not have. As in Prolog, the computation rule is the
leftmost one and the search strategy is depth-first; hence, given
a query
\[
\texttt{:-}\ G_1,\, G_2,\, \dots,\, G_n
\]
the leftmost literal $G_1$ is selected first, and it is resolved with the first
clause whose head unifies with it. Thus, the order of the premises in a rule is the order of the questions. It was
chosen so that the core symptoms, which most often fail, come before the
specifiers and the exclusions; these are skipped altogether once a premise has
certainty $0$ (cf. section~\ref{sec:kb}).

Negative answers do not make a proof fail. An answer ``no'' with certainty $c$
to an item required to be true is treated as a ``yes'' with certainty $1-c$,
and a premise required to be \emph{false} (e.g., ``the disturbance is better
explained by another mental disorder'') contributes the complement of its
certainty. In this way a goal is computed to the end, with a certainty that is
possibly $0$, and the investigation can rank all the goals instead of reporting
only those that succeed. Whether a premise is required true or false
is part of the rule, and the inference engine computes the
certainty accordingly (cf. (\ref{eq:consequent})).
