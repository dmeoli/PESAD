\chapter{Validation}
\label{chap:validation}

The knowledge base is validated against the DSM-5-TR criteria by a suite of
clinical vignettes (the file \code{prolog/clinical\_tests.pl}, run with
\code{swipl prolog/clinical\_tests.pl}), which the continuous integration of the
repository executes at each push. This chapter describes how a case is run and
which kinds of cases the suite contains, then gives the results by uncertainty
method and by case type. Finally, a mutation test measures how many errors
injected into the knowledge base the suite detects.

\section{Method}

A case is a vignette, i.e., the age band of a patient and the answers a
clinician would give, written by me from the criteria of the manual (no case
material of the DSM-5-TR or of a casebook was used), together with a list of
expectations. To run the whole investigation without interaction, the runner first answers
\emph{no} with certainty $1$ to the 238 yes/no items that the engine may pose,
which are discovered by inspecting the clauses of the knowledge base. To the
multiple-choice questions it gives a neutral option that supports no diagnosis
(e.g., a duration of less than 3 days). It then overlays the answers of the
vignette, runs the investigation and checks the expectations on the ranked
goals. A goal is \emph{present} when its certainty is at least $0.5$, the same
threshold used by the evaluation from test results (cf. (\ref{eq:status})). An
expectation states that a goal is present, that it is absent, that it is
present and ranks first, that it ranks first (with any certainty greater than
$0$), or that no goal is present. Each case runs under the six uncertainty methods. For the method
\code{fuzzy\_membership}, the discrete durations and frequencies of the vignette
are converted into numbers (e.g., ``3 to 6 months'' into 4 months), unless the
vignette gives a number. Before the cases, the suite checks that each goal has
its four cases (see below) and its two codes, and that goals, items, questions
and options have a text in both languages.

Table~\ref{tab:case-types} lists the types of the cases. Each of the
92 goals has four cases. In the \emph{typical} case the goal must be present
and rank first. The \emph{subthreshold} case is the typical one with one
symptom, count or duration short of the criterion, and the \emph{exclusion} case
is the typical one with an exclusion criterion that holds (e.g., ``better
explained by another mental disorder'', or a substance etiology); in both the
goal must be absent. The \emph{differential} case is the vignette of a
neighboring disorder, which must rank first while the goal is absent. Other cases test comorbidities (two disorders present together, e.g., panic
disorder and agoraphobia, or ADHD and autism) and specifiers (e.g.,
posttraumatic stress disorder with both specifiers, or with neither). Others
test age rules, e.g., the duration of separation anxiety disorder below and
above 18 years, the single associated symptom of generalized anxiety disorder in
children, the preschool subtype of posttraumatic stress disorder, and the ADHD
threshold at 16 and 17 years. Further cases test cross-chapter differentials
(e.g., autism against social communication disorder, posttraumatic stress
disorder against ADHD) and graded answers (the uncertainty cases, discussed
below), and 13 cases are requests to the evaluation from test results, with the
expected status of goals and criteria, the expected missing data and the
expected errors on invalid requests. Finally, the \emph{boundary} cases place 17
polythetic criteria exactly at their threshold: for an ``at least $M$ of $N$''
criterion, the vignette of the disorder is given exactly $M$ of the items, and
the goal must be present, or $M-1$, and the goal must be absent. These are the
criteria that the typical and subthreshold cases leave away from the threshold
(cf. section~\ref{sec:mutation}).

\label{sec:val-results}

report no error (2754 runs in all). Since passing is a binary outcome, table~\ref{tab:results-method} also

which shows how far the cases are from the threshold of $0.5$. For the typical
cases it gives the mean and the smallest certainty of the goal; for the other
three types, the largest certainty that the goal (which must be absent) reaches.

The margins are wide under all the methods: the typical goals stay above
$0.73$, and the goals that must be absent stay below $0.15$. The exception is
\code{fuzzy\_membership}, in which a duration a little short of the criterion
(e.g., 4 months against ``at least 6 months'') gives a membership of $0.33$
instead of $0$; the goal then reaches $0.32$, still below the threshold. Differences among the t-norms are small in these cases. In fact, the answers of
the vignettes are given with certainty $1$, and the only certainties below $1$
are those of the rules, which are combined through at most three levels. The
minimum gives the largest certainties, as (\ref{eq:order}) predicts, but the
other t-norms are within $0.03$ of it on average. It is worth noting that some exclusion cases do not drive the goal to $0$. When
the exclusion is the conclusion of a rule (e.g., the substance etiology, which
is a parent disorder required false), its certainty is $1 - \cf_{\text{rule}}
\cdot \cf_{\text{ant}}$ by (\ref{eq:consequent}). This is positive if the rule
has certainty below $1$, and a residual certainty of at most $0.15$ remains.

By contrast, the t-norms differ markedly when the answers themselves are uncertain.
Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty-cases} gives the certainty of the expected goal in the
four uncertainty cases. These are panic disorder and posttraumatic stress
disorder with answers of certainty between $0.6$ and $0.9$, a spider phobia that
has lasted 5 months, and a vignette of mild, non-specific work stress (for
which the largest certainty among all the goals is given).

Panic disorder is present under every method, although with the
\L{}ukasiewicz and Einstein t-norms only by a small margin. Posttraumatic stress disorder, whose rule combines many uncertain clusters,
keeps a certainty of $0.659$ under the minimum. It falls below the threshold
with the product ($0.278$), the Hamacher product ($0.398$) and the Einstein
product ($0.185$), where it still ranks first (and the expectation of the case is
set accordingly), and it is $0$ with the \L{}ukasiewicz t-norm, as
table~\ref{tab:tnorms} anticipates for a conjunction of many premises of
certainty $0.8$. This is, in fact, the main argument for keeping the minimum as the
default. For a diagnosis that the manual defines as a conjunction of criteria,
the minimum makes a disorder as certain as its weakest criterion; with the other
t-norms the certainty decreases with the number of criteria, i.e., with a
feature of the encoding (how many premises a rule has) that has no clinical
meaning. The borderline phobia is found only by \code{fuzzy\_membership}, which
grades a five-month duration $0.67$ against the ``typically 6 months or more''
of the criterion, while the discrete methods apply the cut-off. Whether this is
desirable depends on how strictly the word ``typically'' is read; furthermore,
it comes at the price of requesting numbers instead of options.

\section{Mutation Test}
\label{sec:mutation}

A suite in which every case passes may still be unable to detect errors in the
knowledge base, if the cases do not exercise the criteria that are wrong. To
measure this, I generated \emph{mutants} of the knowledge base, each differing
from it by a single error. The errors are of three kinds: (i) the threshold $M$
of an ``at least $M$ of $N$'' criterion raised by $1$; (ii) the same threshold,
when $M \ge 2$, lowered by $1$; (iii) a premise required false (an exclusion, or
a symptom required absent) required true instead. Each mutant was run through
the suite under the method \code{cf}, and it is \emph{killed} when at least one
case fails. The test (\code{make mutate}, i.e., the script \code{tools/mutate.py})
runs the suite once per mutant, and it is not part of the continuous
integration, since it takes several minutes (about 7 with 3 parallel workers on
a desktop machine that was also running other jobs).
Table~\ref{tab:mutation} gives the results.

All the mutants are killed. The inverted exclusions are detected by the typical
cases, since each makes the typical case of the goal fail, and most changes of a
threshold by the typical and subthreshold cases. The boundary cases are needed
for the thresholds that these cases leave away from the boundary. Without them,
17 mutants survived (8 raised and 9 lowered thresholds), for two reasons. A
raised threshold survives when the typical vignette has more items than
required: e.g., the panic disorder vignette has 5 of the 13 panic attack
symptoms, and raising the threshold from 4 to 5 changes nothing. A lowered
threshold survives when the subthreshold case of the goal shortens a different
criterion; e.g., the subthreshold case of agoraphobia removes situations, and
lowering the threshold of the criterion on the fear being almost always provoked
and out of proportion goes unnoticed. Of course, the three kinds of mutation
cover only the errors that are easiest to make in the encoding, and a suite that
kills them all may still miss, e.g., a wrong item in a list, a wrong duration
option or a premise forgotten altogether.
