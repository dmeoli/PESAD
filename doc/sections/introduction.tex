\chapter{Introduction}

This report is concerned with a rule-based expert system that supports the
diagnosis of the disorders of four chapters of the \emph{Diagnostic and
Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders}, fifth edition, text revision
(DSM-5-TR)~\cite{dsm}: the Neurodevelopmental Disorders, the Anxiety Disorders,
the Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders, and the Trauma- and
Stressor-Related Disorders. Given the age band of a patient and the answers of a
clinician about symptoms, durations, onsets and exclusions (or, alternatively,
the scores of standardized tests and interviews), the system has to decide
which of 92 codable diagnostic goals are supported, and with what degree of
certainty. For the goals it cannot settle, it has to say which data are still
missing; for those it supports, which neighboring disorders ought to be ruled
out. Furthermore, it has to justify each conclusion in terms of the criteria of
the manual. The system, called PESAD (Prolog Expert System for Anxiety,
Obsessive-Compulsive and Trauma-Related Disorders, a name that predates the
addition of the neurodevelopmental chapter), is written in
SWI-Prolog~\cite{swipl}, and it can be consulted from a terminal, from a
Telegram bot and from a stateless web API. It is a decision-support tool for a
clinician, not a diagnostic instrument (cf. section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

The setting is the classical one of the knowledge-based systems of the
seventies and eighties. An \emph{expert system}, in the definition of
Feigenbaum~\cite{feigenbaum}, is a program that uses knowledge and inference
procedures to solve problems that would otherwise require a human expert, and
that can explain how a solution was reached. Its usual components are a
knowledge base of facts and production rules, an inference engine that selects
and applies the rules, a working memory holding the facts of the current
consultation, and a user interface, which also carries the explanations of the
reasoning~\cite{jackson,giarratano}. Medicine was among the first fields in
which such systems were tried, MYCIN~\cite{shortliffe-buchanan,mycin} being the
best known example. Its reasons are rather obvious: a diagnostic manual is
largely a set of explicit rules (``at least 3 of the following 6 symptoms, for
at least 6 months, not attributable to a substance''), and a clinician will not
accept a conclusion whose reasons cannot be inspected. Indeed, the DSM-5-TR is well suited to this kind of encoding, since (with a few exceptions) each of its
diagnoses is a conjunction of lettered criteria. Each criterion, in turn, is a
polythetic threshold over a list of items, a duration, an onset, or an exclusion
of the form ``not better explained by another mental disorder''.

What the manual does not provide is a way of reasoning with answers that are
not simply true or false. A clinician may be fairly, but not completely, sure
that a symptom is present, or that it has lasted six months rather than five;
hence, the inference has to propagate a degree of belief from the elementary
answers up to the diagnoses. MYCIN attached a \emph{certainty factor} (CF) to
facts and rules, combining the premises of a rule by their minimum and the rule
with its premises by a product~\cite{shortliffe-buchanan}. This calculus was
later shown to correspond to a probabilistic model only under rather restrictive
independence assumptions~\cite{heckerman}. Yet, it is simple, local and easy to
explain, and for these reasons it was adopted here. In fuzzy logic, the minimum used for a
conjunction is the G\"odel t-norm of fuzzy logic~\cite{zadeh,hajek}, and one may
ask what happens when it is replaced by another triangular norm (t-norm), e.g.,
the product, the \L{}ukasiewicz t-norm~\cite{lukasiewicz}, or the members of the
Hamacher family~\cite{hamacher} known as the Hamacher and the Einstein
products~\cite{klement}. PESAD therefore implements six uncertainty methods,
which share the knowledge base and the rule calculus and differ in the t-norm
(and, for one of them, in the use of graded membership functions over
durations and frequencies). Each clinical case of the validation suite is run under
all of them.

This report describes the system as it stands. The knowledge base encodes the
DSM-5-TR criteria of 92 codable goals (16 anxiety, 22 obsessive-compulsive, 19
trauma-related and 35 neurodevelopmental), where a goal is a disorder together
with the specifier or subtype that makes it a separately codable diagnosis
(e.g., ``Autism Spectrum Disorder, requiring substantial support for social
communication''). Each goal carries its ICD-10-CM code, as printed in the
DSM-5-TR, and its ICD-11 code~\cite{icd11}. Exclusion criteria are premises
required to be false, so that a ``not better explained by'' answer lowers the
certainty of the diagnosis. For each uncertainty method, the validation suite contains 425
cases: for each goal a typical, a subthreshold, an exclusion and a
differential case, plus cases on comorbidities, specifiers, age rules, graded
answers and evaluations from test results. I also report a mutation test, in
which errors are injected into the knowledge base to check that the suite
detects them. Of course, all the cases are vignettes written from the criteria;
passing them shows that the rules agree with the manual as I read it, which is a
much weaker property than clinical validity (cf.
section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

The structure of the report is as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:system} analyzes
the clinical domain, describes how the knowledge was identified and
conceptualized, and presents the implementation of the knowledge base, of the
inference engine with its six uncertainty methods (whose t-norms are defined in
section~\ref{sec:uncertainty}) and of the explanation module.
Chapter~\ref{chap:usage} describes the terminal shell and its commands.
Chapter~\ref{chap:interfaces} describes the engine layer shared by the Telegram
bot and the web API, together with the evaluation of the knowledge base from
test results, which returns lower and upper certainty bounds.
Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} gives the validation suite and its results, by
method and by case type, and the mutation test; finally,
chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions} discusses the limitations of the approach and of
the implementation. Codes of the form \code{F41.1} are ICD-10-CM codes, codes of
the form \code{6B00} are ICD-11 codes, and the identifiers in typewriter font
are the Prolog atoms of the knowledge base.
